The rule to remember

You usually can't close it all on Discord.

Discord is where you find people and start the conversation — not where you finish it. The folks you DM can point you to the real decision, but the close almost always happens elsewhere. Use Discord to open the door, then walk through it on another channel.

X — reply to their threads LinkedIn — make it professional Email — follow up + materials Phone / call — the real conversation

Why Discord is different

Nobody says "hope this message finds you well" here. People type lowercase, skip punctuation, and build in public. The admins, maintainers, and heavy users are all in the same channels — and they reply.

The channel-first strategy

Don't cold-DM strangers out of nowhere. The best leads come from being visible in channels first.

  1. Start discussions. Ask genuine questions in channels — which model people use for a task, how they're handling costs, what they think of a new release.
  2. Spot who replies. The person who actually knows the answer is very often an admin, a maintainer, or the person who runs the project or site.
  3. DM that person. Not to pitch — to keep the conversation going. "hey, that answer was super helpful, mind if I ask a couple more things?"
  4. Ask questions in the DM. Learn what they run, what they're wrestling with, what they're already using.
  5. Bridge to value only when it's natural. If what you offer solves their actual problem, mention it. Casually.

Do your homework first

Before you DM anyone, know who they are. A little research turns a cold DM into a relevant one.

  • Are they a top spender in the niche? (often visible on leaderboards or rankings)
  • Are they an admin or moderator?
  • Do they run a project, site, or community?
  • What are they already using?
Why it matters: when you reference something specific about them, the DM instantly stops feeling like spam.

DM etiquette (non-negotiable)

  • Read the server rules before you message anyone.
  • Acknowledge you're DMing — "idk if this is allowed to DM but..." shows respect for the space.
  • Never mass-DM. One real person at a time.
  • Lowercase, human, zero corporate speak. If it reads like a bot, it's ignored.

Graceful exits

Rejection is normal. End warm so the door stays open.

The whole reply is just: "no worries! thanks for the response!" — no pushback, no wall of text, no follow-up pitch. Casual in, casual out.

What casual actually looks like

Pulled from real conversations — anonymized, no usernames, just the parts worth copying.

The opener

You
hello, idk if this is allowed or not (dm) but have you looked at [competitor] alternatives? maybe something cheaper and something that gives you [concrete benefit]?
Why it works: etiquette first ("idk if this is allowed"), specific and relevant (their existing tool + a real benefit), and it reads like a human, not a bot.

The research line

You
gotcha, you're still listed as one of the top spenders in the niche, so was wondering...
Why it works: proves you did your homework, and it flatters with fact — not fluff.

The value prop

You
i work at a similar company and we offer a program where you get 5% token spend back on your user spend — worth exploring on a call or chat?
Why it works: one concrete number (5% back), plus a soft CTA. No pressure, no "let's sync calendars."

The graceful exit

Them
Ah I see. No, I'm good thanks.
You
no worries! thanks for the response!
Why it works: zero pushback. Leaves the door open for a future touch — you never know when they'll remember you.

The "help first" play

You
ahh gotcha gotcha, that makes sense. appreciate you being down to add the login flow. i'll poke around on the [product] side for the other stuff and see where it goes. thanks for the help!
Why it works: you helped them with something real first. Warm, collaborative, no pitch. Even if it doesn't close now, you've built trust for the next touch.
